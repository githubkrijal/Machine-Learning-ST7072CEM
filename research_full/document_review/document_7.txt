Introduction
Forest fires are a recurring environmental hazard in
Nepal, especially during the dry pre-monsoon sea-
son. They damage forests and biodiversity, reduce
air quality, release carbon, and threaten nearby
communities. Satellite fire observations record past
incidents, while weather data describe conditions
that influence fuel drying, ignition, and fire spread.
Combining these sources provides an opportunity
to estimate daily forest-fire risk using machine
learning.
Nepal’s steep terrain, varied climate, and limited
access to remote forests can make conventional
monitoring and rapid response difficult. Fire occur-
rence also changes by season and ecological region.
A data-driven approach can examine these spatial
and temporal differences consistently. Historical
observations can be used to learn relationships be-
tween environmental conditions and recorded fire
events, producing risk estimates that may support
monitoring and preparedness.
This proposal presents a machine-learning frame-
work that combines ICIMOD active-fire observa-
tions with ERA5-Land weather information and
relevant spatial data. The study focuses on daily
fire occurrence between February 2012 and June
2016. It will compare interpretable and nonlinear
classification models and assess whether weather
conditions provide useful predictive information
for Nepal.
Problem Statement
Historical fire records show where fires were de-
tected but do not predict risk for a new location
or date. Fire observations must be matched with
temperature, moisture, rainfall, and wind infor-
mation by location and time. The data are also
imbalanced because non-fire observations greatly
outnumber recorded fires. A suitable method must
prevent data leakage, address class imbalance, and
evaluate missed fires as well as false alarms.
Several technical problems must therefore be ad-
dressed. Satellite detections can include repeated
observations of the same event, while cloud cover
and satellite overpass timing may cause some fires
to be missed. Weather data use regular grid cells
that do not exactly match fire coordinates, and
hourly measurements must be transformed into
meaningful daily and recent-weather indicators.
Randomly dividing observations could allow closely
related dates to appear in both training and testing
data, resulting in an overly optimistic evaluation.
The proposed approach will use chronological data
partitions and metrics appropriate for an imbal-
anced classification problem.
Objective
The main objective is to develop and evaluate a
machine-learning model for daily forest-fire occur-
rence in Nepal. The study will:
• combine ICIMOD fire records with weather and
spatial data;
•derive daily and recent-weather predictors;
• compare Logistic Regression, Random Forest,
and Gradient Boosting; and
• evaluate performance and identify influential
predictors.
Proposed Methodology
The proposed workflow is shown in Figure 1. It
separates data preparation, feature engineering,
model development, temporal validation, and final
outputs so that each stage can be checked and
reproduced.
1
Figure 1: Proposed forest-fire risk prediction
workflow.
1. Data preparation and integration.Coor-
dinates and acquisition dates will be checked for
missing, invalid, or duplicate records. Fire observa-
tions will be assigned to the nearest weather grid
cell and date. Temperature and dewpoint will be
used to estimate atmospheric moisture, the two
wind components will be combined to calculate
wind speed, and precipitation will be converted
to millimetres. Hourly observations will then be
summarized into suitable daily indicators.
2. Feature engineering and target construc-
tion.Daily mean, minimum, maximum, and ac-
cumulated weather indicators will be produced as
appropriate. Rolling rainfall totals, previous-day
temperature, recent wind conditions, consecutive
dry days, month, and season will be considered.
A value of 1 will represent a grid-cell day with a
recorded fire. Sampled forested grid-cell days with-
out a recorded detection will form the comparison
class. Sampling and class weighting will be applied
only to the training data.
3. Model development.Logistic Regression will
provide a transparent baseline. Random Forest will
capture nonlinear relationships and interactions,
and Gradient Boosting will test whether sequential
tree learning improves prediction. Data from 2012–
2014 will be used for training, 2015 for validation
and model selection, and January–June 2016 for
final testing. This chronological split will reduce
leakage from future dates.
4. Evaluation and interpretation.Accuracy
will not be used alone because non-fire observa-
tions are expected to outnumber fire observations.
Precision will measure the reliability of positive pre-
dictions, while recall will measure the proportion
of recorded fires identified. F1-score, ROC-AUC,
precision–recall curves, and confusion matrices will
provide complementary evidence. Feature impor-
tance and mapped predictions will help explain
the final model.
Data Source
ICIMOD active-fire incidents:13,781 records
dated 1 February 2012–19 June 2016, including
coordinates, dates, and land-cover information.
ERA5-Land:Daily or hourly 2-metre tempera-
ture, 2-metre dewpoint temperature, total precipi-
tation, and 10-metre U and V wind components for
Nepal. A Nepal boundary and available elevation
or forest-cover layers will provide spatial context.
The weather data should cover at least 25 Jan-
uary 2012–19 June 2016 so that seven-day lagged
features can be calculated for the earliest fire obser-
vations. The study area will use Nepal’s national
boundary or, when necessary, the approximate
bounding box 26.0–30.5 degrees north and 80.0–
88.5 degrees east. The data will be downloaded
in manageable yearly batches and then combined
after checking variables, dates, units, coordinate
orientation, and spatial resolution.
Expected Output
The project will produce a cleaned fire–weather
dataset, engineered predictors, three trained
classification models, and a comparison of their
performance. Expected presentation outputs
include confusion matrices, evaluation curves,
feature-importance charts, and maps of estimated
daily fire risk. The final report will describe the
method, findings, limitations, and recommenda-
tions.
The principal analytical output will be a daily
probability or risk score for each eligible location.
Results will identify the model that performs best
on unseen dates and the environmental features
most strongly associated with recorded fire oc-
currence. The project will also provide reusable
Python processing steps so that additional years
of weather and fire observations can be incorpo-
rated later. The outputs are intended for academic
analysis; operational warnings would require fur-
ther validation with near-real-time observations
and consultation with relevant authorities.
